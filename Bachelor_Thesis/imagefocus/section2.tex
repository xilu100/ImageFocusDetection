\section{Theoretical Background}
\subsection{Image Focus and Blur Fundamentals}
\subsubsection{Concept of Focus and Blur}
\par The focus state of an image is usually reflected by the degree to which
edges, textures, and high-frequency information are preserved in local regions.
When the imaging system is accurately focused on the target plane, boundaries
and details in the image can be presented clearly. In contrast, when the lens
is out of focus or relative motion occurs between the camera and the object,
the image shows different degrees of blur. Common types of blur include
defocus blur and motion blur. Defocus blur usually results from the limitation
of depth of field or the shift of the focal plane, while motion blur is mainly
caused by relative displacement during exposure. Although their generation
mechanisms are different, both may lead to local edge diffusion, texture
weakening, and loss of high-frequency components
\cite{pertuz2013analysis,yi2016lbp,fergus2006removing}.

\par From the perspective of image features, clear regions usually have more
obvious grayscale variation, stronger local gradients, and richer texture
structures, while blurred regions often show smoother edge transitions,
smoothed details, and a frequency distribution more concentrated in the
low-frequency range. Therefore, the essence of focus detection can be
understood as the analysis of the fidelity of structural information in local
regions. For the patch-level classification task studied in this thesis, how to
effectively describe the difference between clear and blurred regions
\cite{pertuz2013analysis,5739529} is the theoretical basis for subsequent
feature design and model learning.
\subsubsection{Imaging Degradation Model}
\par From the perspective of the imaging degradation model, a blurred image can
usually be expressed as the convolution of a clear image and a point spread
function, plus a noise term \cite{lam2000iterative}, namely
\begin{equation}
    g(x,y)=f(x,y)*h(x,y)+n(x,y)
    \label{eq:blur-model}
\end{equation}
\par where $f(x,y)$ denotes the ideal clear image, $g(x,y)$ denotes the
observed image, $h(x,y)$ denotes the blur kernel or point spread function,
$n(x,y)$ denotes noise, and the symbol $*$ represents convolution. Let
$F(u,v)$, $G(u,v)$, $H(u,v)$, and $N(u,v)$ denote the two-dimensional Fourier
transforms of $f(x,y)$, $g(x,y)$, $h(x,y)$, and $n(x,y)$, respectively. By the
convolution theorem, convolution in the spatial domain becomes pointwise
multiplication in the frequency domain. Therefore, Eq.~\eqref{eq:blur-model}
can be written in the frequency domain as
\begin{equation}
    G(u,v)=F(u,v)H(u,v)+N(u,v)
    \label{eq:blur-model-frequency}
\end{equation}
\par Equation~\eqref{eq:blur-model-frequency} is mathematically equivalent to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:blur-model}; the difference is only that the same degradation
process is expressed in the frequency domain rather than in the spatial
domain. This shows that the blur process essentially suppresses the high-frequency
components of an image. As a result, details such as edges and textures are
weakened by defocus or motion, which also provides the theoretical basis for
the construction of subsequent sharpness features \cite{lam2000iterative}.

\subsection{Patch-Based Image Representation}
\par In image analysis tasks, using the whole image directly as input can retain
global context, but it is not suitable for describing spatial changes in local
focus states. In contrast, dividing the image into multiple local patches of
fixed size can transform a complex region detection problem into a group of
local classification problems with consistent structure and easier modeling
\cite{Hou_2016_CVPR}. Each patch corresponds to one grid cell in the original
image, so the image can be represented as a regular two-dimensional patch set.

\par This representation has two main advantages. First, patch-based
representation can highlight local texture, gradient, and frequency
information, allowing the model to focus more directly on local visual features
related to focus state. Second, fixed-size patches make it convenient to
extract statistical features in a unified way or to construct the input of
neural networks, thus reducing the complexity of model design and training
procedures \cite{Hou_2016_CVPR,dosovitskiy2020image}.

\par Let the grayscale input image be denoted by $I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}$, and let
the patch side length be $p$. When the image size is divisible by $p$, it can
be divided into
\begin{equation}
    M=\frac{H}{p},\qquad N=\frac{W}{p}
    \label{eq:patch-grid}
\end{equation}
\par row and column grids, and the $(i,j)$-th patch can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    P_{i,j}=I[ip:(i+1)p,\; jp:(j+1)p],\qquad
    0\le i<M,\; 0\le j<N
    \label{eq:patch-definition}
\end{equation}
\par Therefore, the whole image can be written as the patch set
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}=\left\{P_{i,j}\mid 0\le i<M,\;0\le j<N\right\}
    \label{eq:patch-set}
\end{equation}
\par This representation transforms the original image analysis task into a
patch-by-patch modeling problem over the local patch set $\mathcal{P}$. The
above derivation assumes that the input size is compatible with the patch size.
If the original image size is not divisible by $p$, appropriate adjustment is
required in advance.

\subsection{Patch Representations and Automatic Labeling}
\subsubsection{Two Types of Patch Representation}
\par In image focus analysis, the representation of local patches can be roughly
divided into two categories. The first category is focus measures based on
hand-crafted sharpness indicators \cite{5739529,1284395}, whose purpose is to
directly describe the sharpness level of local regions by using edge, gradient,
and frequency-domain statistics. The second category is raw representation
based on pixel values, that is, unfolding the grayscale patch matrix into a
vector and then letting the classifier or dimensionality reduction method model
it automatically \cite{murphy2022pml,goodfellow2016deep}. The former has
strong interpretability and is suitable for explicit sharpness scoring, while
the latter preserves more complete local intensity distribution information and
is therefore a more natural input form for supervised classifiers and CNN-based
patch models \cite{murphy2022pml,goodfellow2016deep,Hou_2016_CVPR}. In this
thesis, hand-crafted focus measures such as Laplacian, Sobel, and
frequency-based statistics are also used to construct sharpness scores and
derive supervisory labels. Therefore, the labeling stage is closer to
rule-based automatic labeling than to model-bootstrapped pseudo-labeling
\cite{lee2013pseudo,5739529,1284395}. These labels then provide supervision for
subsequent classifiers, which learn the final clear/blur decision boundary from
patch data rather than relying only on fixed heuristic thresholds
\cite{murphy2022pml,goodfellow2016deep,yi2016lbp,Hou_2016_CVPR}.

\subsubsection{Focus Measures in Spatial and Frequency Domains}
\par Laplacian features reflect second-order grayscale variation and are
sensitive to edge and detail changes, so they are often used to measure local
sharpness \cite{pertuz2013analysis}. Sobel features describe the gradient strength in the horizontal and
vertical directions through first-order derivative approximation and can
represent local edge saliency well \cite{pertuz2013analysis}. Fast Fourier
Transform (FFT) features analyze image signals from the
frequency-domain perspective and use the proportion or energy of high-frequency
components as an important indicator of sharpness \cite{yap2004image},
because clear regions usually preserve more high-frequency details
\cite{pertuz2013analysis}. Combining
these features makes it possible to describe the focus state of a patch from
both the spatial and frequency domains. Compared with a single feature, fused
features \cite{pertuz2013analysis,yap2004image} usually have better stability
and are also more suitable for subsequent automatic label generation.

\par For a local grayscale patch $P(x,y)$, the Laplacian operator can be
written as
\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2 P=\frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial y^2}
    \label{eq:laplacian-basic}
\end{equation}
\par A common local sharpness measure is the variance of the Laplacian response
\cite{pertuz2013analysis}:
\begin{equation}
    s_{\mathrm{lap}}(P)=\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\nabla^2 P\right)
    \label{eq:laplacian-var-basic}
\end{equation}
\par Sobel features approximate the local gradient by first-order differences in
two directions:
\begin{equation}
    G_x=S_x*P,\qquad G_y=S_y*P
    \label{eq:sobel-basic}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    G(x,y)=\sqrt{G_x(x,y)^2+G_y(x,y)^2}
    \label{eq:sobel-magnitude-basic}
\end{equation}
\par Therefore, the average gradient strength of a patch can be written as
\begin{equation}
    s_{\mathrm{sob}}(P)=\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{(x,y)\in P} G(x,y)
    \label{eq:sobel-score-basic}
\end{equation}
\par In the frequency domain, the two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform can
be written as follows and is typically computed efficiently by FFT algorithms
\cite{cooley1965algorithm}:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{P}(u,v)=\sum_{x=0}^{m-1}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}
    P(x,y)\exp\!\left[-j2\pi\left(\frac{ux}{m}+\frac{vy}{n}\right)\right]
    \label{eq:dft-basic}
\end{equation}
\par If $\Omega_h$ denotes the high-frequency region, a high-frequency ratio can
be further constructed. Taking the energy ratio as an example, it can be
defined as
\begin{equation}
    s_{\mathrm{fft}}(P)=
    \frac{\sum_{(u,v)\in\Omega_h}\left|\hat{P}(u,v)\right|^2}
    {\sum_{u}\sum_{v}\left|\hat{P}(u,v)\right|^2}
    \label{eq:fft-energy-basic}
\end{equation}
\par After combining the three types of features, the feature vector used for
subsequent sharpness analysis can be obtained as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}(P)=
    \left[s_{\mathrm{lap}}(P),\; s_{\mathrm{sob}}(P),\; s_{\mathrm{fft}}(P)\right]^\top
    \label{eq:feature-vector-basic}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Pixel-Level Vector Representation}
\par Complementary to the above statistical features is the direct use of raw
patch pixels to construct the input for supervised learning. The vectorized
representation of a $p\times p$ patch can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}(P)=\operatorname{vec}(P)\in\mathbb{R}^{p^2}
    \label{eq:pixel-vector-basic}
\end{equation}
\par If the grayscale values are further normalized to the interval $[0,1]$,
then we obtain
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{z}}(P)=\frac{1}{255}\,\mathbf{z}(P)
    \label{eq:pixel-vector-normalized}
\end{equation}
\par This representation does not explicitly specify which edge or frequency
statistics are most important. Instead, it preserves the complete local
distribution of all pixels within the patch, and the subsequent classifier or
convolutional network learns effective discriminative features by itself. The
dimensionality of the raw pixel vector is $p^2$, which grows quadratically
with the patch size $p$. Therefore, dimensionality reduction methods
\cite{murphy2022pml} are often combined in practical modeling to control the
complexity of the feature space.

\subsection{Classical Machine Learning Methods}
\subsubsection{General Characteristics of Classical Models}
\par Image classification tasks based on patch vector representation can often be
implemented with classical machine learning methods \cite{murphy2022pml}.
Compared with end-to-end deep learning, these methods do not automatically
learn hierarchical representations in multi-layer networks, but rely on input
feature vectors for discrimination. Their advantages lie in relatively simple
model structure, lower training cost, and often better controllability in
small-sample scenarios \cite{cortes1995support,breiman2001random}.

\par The typical classical classifiers involved in this thesis include support
vector machines, decision trees, and random forests.

\subsubsection{Support Vector Machines, Decision Trees, Random Forests, and PCA}
\paragraph{Support Vector Machines.} A support vector machine (SVM) is a
supervised classifier that constructs a decision boundary by maximizing the
margin between classes \cite{cortes1995support}. For a binary classification
problem based on the feature vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, its linear
decision function is usually written as
\begin{equation}
    f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b
    \label{eq:svm-decision-basic}
\end{equation}
\par Here, $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ define the separating hyperplane. SVMs are
particularly suitable for small- and medium-scale datasets and for feature
spaces of relatively high dimensionality, because their optimization objective
focuses directly on the discriminative boundary rather than on modeling the
full data distribution \cite{cortes1995support,scholkopf2002learning}. In the
soft-margin setting, the classifier allows a limited number of margin
violations in order to improve robustness to non-separable data. The
corresponding optimization problem can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\mathbf{w},b,\boldsymbol{\xi}}
    \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2+C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_i
    \label{eq:svm-objective-basic}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{s.t.}\quad
    y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b)\ge 1-\xi_i,\qquad
    \xi_i\ge 0
    \label{eq:svm-constraint-basic}
\end{equation}
\par In this formulation, $\xi_i$ is a slack variable that quantifies the
degree to which the $i$-th sample violates the margin constraint, and the
parameter $C$ controls the trade-off between margin maximization and training
error, while $n$ denotes the number of training samples. A larger $C$
emphasizes fitting the training data more strictly,
whereas a smaller $C$ imposes stronger regularization, that is, a stronger
preference for a simpler decision boundary, and permits a wider
margin. The samples lying closest to the separating boundary are called support
vectors, and they largely determine the final classifier
\cite{cortes1995support,scholkopf2002learning}. For the patch classification
task studied in this thesis, SVM provides a theoretically well-founded baseline
for learning a clear/blur decision boundary from either handcrafted features or
dimension-reduced pixel representations.

\par Beyond the linear case, support vector machines can also describe
nonlinear class boundaries through kernel methods, which use kernel functions
to measure similarity between samples in an implicitly transformed feature
space. However, standard kernel SVM usually needs to process a large kernel
matrix explicitly or implicitly,
and its training and storage cost increases significantly as the number of
samples grows. To alleviate this problem, kernel approximation methods are
often used to transform the originally expensive kernel computation into a
finite-dimensional feature mapping. Among them, the Nystr\"{o}m method is a
typical low-rank kernel approximation strategy. In essence, it selects a
subset of representative samples and uses them to approximate the original
large kernel matrix, that is, the matrix of pairwise kernel similarities,
by a lower-rank matrix. The resulting approximation makes
it possible to construct an explicit feature mapping $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ such
that the kernel function satisfies
\begin{equation}
    k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')\approx \phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x}')
    \label{eq:nystrom-basic}
\end{equation}
\par Here, $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ denotes the explicit feature mapping induced by
the approximation. In this way, a nonlinear classification problem that
originally relied on the kernel trick can be approximately transformed into a
linear classification problem in an explicit feature space, which reduces computational complexity
while retaining certain nonlinear expressive ability. For the task of this
thesis, this idea provides the theoretical basis for the subsequent use of SVM
based on Nystr\"{o}m kernel approximation
\cite{williams2001using,scholkopf2002learning}.

\paragraph{Decision Trees.} A decision tree is a supervised learning model
that performs classification by recursively partitioning the feature space into
a sequence of decision regions \cite{breiman2017classification}. Each internal
node corresponds to a feature test, each branch corresponds to a possible
outcome of that test, and each leaf node gives the predicted class. A common
splitting strategy is to select the feature that yields the largest reduction
in node impurity. Using entropy as the impurity measure, the corresponding
information gain can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Gain}(D,a)=H(D)-\sum_{v=1}^{V}\frac{|D^{(v)}|}{|D|}H(D^{(v)})
    \label{eq:info-gain-basic}
\end{equation}
\par where $H(D)$ denotes the information entropy of the sample set $D$, and
$D^{(v)}$ denotes the subset obtained according to the $v$-th value of feature
$a$. Here, $V$ denotes the number of branches induced by feature $a$, and
entropy measures the class uncertainty of a node: higher entropy indicates
that samples from different classes are more mixed, whereas lower entropy
indicates a purer node. Thus, node impurity describes how mixed the class
labels are within a node. This recursive splitting process allows decision
trees to model nonlinear
decision boundaries and feature interactions without requiring explicit feature
transformation. However, a single tree is often sensitive to local data
variation and may overfit the training set, that is, fit training samples too
closely while generalizing poorly to unseen data, if it grows too deep
\cite{breiman2017classification}. In the context of this thesis, decision trees
provide an interpretable baseline for analyzing how handcrafted sharpness
features or reduced patch representations are separated into clear and blurred
categories.

\paragraph{Random Forests.} A random forest is an ensemble learning method that
combines multiple decision trees in order to improve predictive stability and
generalization \cite{breiman2001random}. It typically uses bootstrap sampling
and random feature selection, meaning that each split considers only a random
subset of the available features, so that each tree is trained on a different
view of the data. Its output can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}=\operatorname{mode}\left\{T_1(\mathbf{x}),T_2(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,T_K(\mathbf{x})\right\}
    \label{eq:random-forest-basic}
\end{equation}
\par where $T_k(\mathbf{x})$ denotes the prediction result of the $k$-th
decision tree, $K$ denotes the number of trees in the forest, and
$\operatorname{mode}$ denotes majority voting. Because the
final decision aggregates multiple weakly correlated trees, random forests
usually achieve better robustness than a single decision tree, especially when
the data distribution is noisy or contains diverse local patterns
\cite{breiman2001random}. For the patch-level focus classification task, this
mechanism is useful because it can reduce the variance of a single tree and
provide a more stable prediction
under local texture variation and feature noise.

\paragraph{Principal Component Analysis and Preprocessing.} Since the
dimensionality of vectorized patches is $p^2$ and therefore grows quadratically
with the patch size $p$, classical models often need to combine procedures such
as feature standardization, kernel approximation, or principal component
analysis to reduce training complexity while preserving the main information.
When the input is a high-dimensional pixel vector, principal component analysis
is also commonly used to map the original features into a low-dimensional
subspace. Let the sample vector be $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, then
the dimensionality-reduced representation of principal component analysis
(PCA) \cite{perry2025inference,zheng2021application} can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{r}=W^\top(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}),\qquad
    W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k},\; k<d
    \label{eq:pca-basic}
\end{equation}
\par where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ denotes the mean of the training samples, and $W$
is composed of the first $k$ principal component directions, that is, the
directions along which the data variance is largest. Here, $k$ denotes
the target dimension after projection. In essence, PCA projects the original
data onto orthogonal directions with the largest variance, so that redundant
correlations among pixel dimensions can be
compressed into a smaller number of informative components. The role of PCA is
therefore to compress redundant dimensions, reduce the influence of noise, and
provide a more compact representation for subsequent classifiers, as commonly
motivated in PCA-based dimensionality reduction
\cite{perry2025inference,zheng2021application}. In this thesis, PCA serves as
a practical dimensionality-reduction step when vectorized pixel patches are
used as model input, so that the main variation structure can be retained while
the complexity of subsequent classification is reduced.

\par In classical machine learning pipelines, in addition to dimensionality
reduction, input features are often standardized so that different dimensions
have similar numerical scales. If one feature dimension is denoted by $x_j$,
its standardized result can be written as
\begin{equation}
    x'_j=\frac{x_j-\mu_j}{\sigma_j}
    \label{eq:standardization-basic}
\end{equation}
\par where $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ denote the mean and standard deviation of the
$j$-th feature dimension on the training set, respectively. Standardization
prevents large-scale features from dominating too strongly during distance
calculation, margin optimization, or kernel approximation, thereby improving
the training stability of subsequent linear classifiers and kernel methods. For
models such as support vector machines that are sensitive to feature scale,
standardization \cite{hsu2003practical} is usually an important preprocessing
step to ensure that information from different dimensions can be used in a
more balanced way.

\subsection{Convolutional Neural Networks for Image Analysis}
\subsubsection{Advantages of CNNs for Patch Analysis}
\par Convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
\cite{lecun2002gradient,krizhevsky2012imagenet} are a class of neural networks
specifically designed for grid-structured data such as images. Their core
advantage is that they can automatically learn multi-level feature
representations directly from raw pixels, without relying completely on
hand-crafted features. Through mechanisms such as convolution, nonlinear
activation, and pooling, CNNs can gradually extract hierarchical information
ranging from local edges to higher-level semantic structures, and they have
therefore been widely applied in tasks such as classification, detection, and
segmentation. Here, pooling summarizes local responses and reduces spatial
resolution while retaining salient information.

\par For image focus detection, a key advantage of CNNs is that they can
automatically learn discriminative patterns related to sharpness, such as
complex combinations among edge strength, texture structure, and local
contrast. Especially under the patch-based setting, CNNs can directly take
local image patches as input and avoid the information loss that may arise in
manual feature selection \cite{lecun2002gradient,krizhevsky2012imagenet}.
However, deep models usually require more training data to perform stably
\cite{goodfellow2016deep,shorten2019survey}. When the sample size is limited,
complex networks are prone to overfitting \cite{goodfellow2016deep}.
Therefore, in small-sample research, using a lightweight
CNN \cite{howard2017mobilenets} with fewer parameters and a shallower structure
is a reasonable choice to balance model representation ability and training
feasibility under limited-data conditions \cite{goodfellow2016deep,shorten2019survey}.

\subsubsection{Basic Operations of CNNs}
\par Artificial neural networks are learning models inspired by the way
biological neurons are abstractly connected in layered structures. In a neural
network, each layer receives an input, applies a weighted linear combination
followed by a nonlinear activation, and passes the result to the next layer
\cite{goodfellow2016deep,lecun2002gradient}. By stacking multiple such layers,
the model can gradually transform raw input data into more discriminative
representations for classification or regression tasks.

\par From the perspective of supervised learning, the main advantage of neural
networks is that they can jointly learn both feature representation and
decision rules directly from data. Instead of manually specifying all relevant
features in advance, the network adjusts its parameters by minimizing a loss
function on training samples, so that useful patterns can be extracted
automatically \cite{goodfellow2016deep}. This property is especially important
in image analysis, where discriminative information is often distributed across
many local structures and is difficult to describe completely by fixed
hand-crafted rules.

\par A standard feedforward neural network is usually organized as an input
layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. Each hidden layer
performs a nonlinear transformation on the previous representation, so that the
network can model complex relationships between the input and the target
\cite{goodfellow2016deep}. During training, the parameters of all layers are
updated through gradient-based optimization in order to reduce the prediction
error on the training set. As a result, the network gradually learns internal
representations that are useful for the final task.

\par When neural networks are applied to image data, however, a fully connected
architecture is often inefficient because the number of parameters grows
rapidly with the input size and local spatial structure is not explicitly
exploited. Convolutional neural networks address this issue by introducing
local receptive fields and weight sharing, so that the same kernel can be
applied across different spatial positions \cite{lecun2002gradient}. This
design greatly reduces the parameter count and makes CNNs more suitable for
learning local patterns such as edges, textures, and shape-related structures
from image patches.

\par If the input patch is denoted by $P\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}$, then the
input tensor of the CNN can be written as
\begin{equation}
    X(P)\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p\times p}
    \label{eq:cnn-input-basic}
\end{equation}
\par Here, $X(P)$ denotes the tensor form of patch $P$ in channel-first format,
that is, a single-channel dimension is added on the basis of the grayscale
image. In mathematical form, the basic operation of a convolutional layer can
then be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    z_{i,j}^{(k)}=
    \sum_{c=1}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}}\sum_{m}\sum_{n}
    w_{m,n}^{(k,c)}x_{i+m,j+n}^{(c)}+b^{(k)}
    \label{eq:cnn-conv-basic}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    a_{i,j}^{(k)}=\sigma\!\left(z_{i,j}^{(k)}\right)
    \label{eq:cnn-activation-basic}
\end{equation}
\par Here, $z_{i,j}^{(k)}$ denotes the pre-activation response at spatial
position $(i,j)$ of the $k$-th output feature map, and $a_{i,j}^{(k)}$ denotes
the corresponding activated output. In addition, $x^{(c)}$ denotes the $c$-th
input channel, $w^{(k,c)}$ denotes the weight of the $k$-th convolution kernel
on the $c$-th channel, and $b^{(k)}$ denotes the bias term. Here, a
convolution kernel is a small learnable filter that slides across the input to
extract local patterns, $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the nonlinear activation
function, and $C_{\mathrm{in}}$ denotes the number of input channels. After
several layers of convolution and pooling, the network finally
outputs class probabilities through softmax:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_c=
    \frac{\exp(o_c)}{\sum_{t=1}^{M}\exp(o_t)}
    \label{eq:softmax-basic}
\end{equation}
\par The corresponding classification loss usually takes the form of
cross-entropy:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}=
    -\sum_{c=1}^{M} y_c\log \hat{p}_c
    \label{eq:cross-entropy-basic}
\end{equation}
\par where $\hat{p}_c$ denotes the predicted probability of class $c$, $o_c$
denotes the pre-softmax output score of the model for class $c$, $y_c$
denotes the one-hot encoding of the true label, and $\mathcal{L}$ denotes the
cross-entropy loss. Here, $M$ denotes the number of classes.

\par By minimizing the loss function, the CNN can learn feature
representations suitable for focus state discrimination in an end-to-end
manner.

\par At inference time, the final class label is usually obtained by selecting
the class with the largest output score. This decision rule can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}=\arg\max_{c\in\{0,\dots,M-1\}} o_c
    \label{eq:argmax-basic}
\end{equation}
\par For binary classification, the decision can also be made by comparing the
predicted probability of one class with a threshold. Let the clear class be
class $1$, let $\hat{p}_1$ denote its predicted probability, and let $\tau$
denote the decision threshold. Then
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}=
    \begin{cases}
        1, & \hat{p}_1\ge \tau, \\
        0, & \hat{p}_1<\tau,
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:binary-threshold-basic}
\end{equation}
\par This threshold-based form makes it possible to adjust the trade-off
between the precision and recall of the clear class, rather than relying only
on a fixed maximum-score decision rule \cite{davis2006relationship}. Such a
mechanism is especially useful when the class distribution is imbalanced and
the research focus is more concerned with the detection ability of clear
regions.

\subsection{Learning under Limited Data Conditions}
\subsubsection{Challenges of Limited Data}
\par Small-sample learning is a common issue in many practical image tasks,
especially when the cost of local annotation is high \cite{shu2018small}. When the available
training data are insufficient, the model often finds it difficult to learn
stable class boundaries and tends to overfit accidental patterns in the
training samples, thereby reducing its generalization ability on new samples.
In this context, a validation set refers to a held-out subset that is not used
to update model parameters, but is used to monitor generalization performance
and support model selection during training. This problem exists for both
classical methods and deep learning methods, but
its form is different. Classical methods are more easily limited by feature
representation ability, while deep models are more easily limited by the
mismatch between parameter scale and data demand \cite{goodfellow2016deep}.

\par To alleviate the influence of limited data, common strategies in research
include data augmentation, class reweighting, model lightweighting, and
controlling experimental variables \cite{shorten2019survey,he2009learning,howard2017mobilenets}.
Data augmentation expands the training distribution by applying perturbations
to the original samples and helps the model obtain stronger robustness
\cite{shorten2019survey}. Class reweighting can alleviate the class imbalance
problem \cite{he2009learning}. Model lightweighting reduces the risk of
overfitting by decreasing the number of parameters
\cite{howard2017mobilenets}. For this thesis, the low-sample condition
is not only an experimental limitation, but also the core premise for
comparing the applicability of different methods. Therefore, understanding the
learning characteristics under limited data conditions is a necessary
theoretical basis for analyzing the performance differences between classical
machine learning and lightweight CNNs.

\par When the training data are also accompanied by imbalanced class
distribution, in addition to directly introducing class weights into the loss
function, the frequency of training samples can also be adjusted through
resampling strategies. The former increases the proportion of minority-class
errors in the optimization objective so that the model pays more attention to
categories that are difficult to cover during parameter updates. The latter
changes the class composition in mini-batches or in the training set, so that
minority-class samples are seen more frequently during training. Both methods
aim to alleviate the bias caused by class imbalance, but their mechanisms are
not the same \cite{Cui_2019_CVPR,buda2018systematic}. Reweighting mainly acts
at the loss level, while resampling acts at the data sampling distribution
level.

\subsubsection{Risk Minimization and Weighted Learning}
\par From the perspective of statistical learning, model training can usually be
viewed as an empirical risk minimization problem \cite{murphy2022pml}:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\theta}\;
    \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell\!\left(f(\mathbf{x}_i;\theta),y_i\right)
    +\lambda\,\Omega(\theta)
    \label{eq:erm-basic}
\end{equation}
\par where $N$ denotes the number of training samples, $\ell(\cdot)$ denotes
the loss function, $\Omega(\theta)$ denotes the regularization term, and
$\lambda$ is the regularization coefficient. Here, $\theta$ denotes the set of
trainable model parameters, $f(\mathbf{x}_i;\theta)$ denotes the model output
for the $i$-th sample, and empirical risk minimization means minimizing the
average loss measured on the training set. The regularization term
$\Omega(\theta)$ is added to penalize overly complex models. When $N$ is
small, the empirical risk becomes less representative of the overall
distribution, and the model is more likely to memorize accidental noise in the
training samples, which leads to low training error but high generalization
error. To alleviate the influence of class imbalance, weighted loss is also
often introduced in practical
training:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{w}=
    -\sum_{c=1}^{M}\alpha_c\,y_c\log \hat{p}_c
    \label{eq:weighted-loss-basic}
\end{equation}
\par where $\mathcal{L}_{w}$ denotes the weighted loss, $\alpha_c$ denotes the
weight assigned to class $c$, and $M$ denotes the number of classes. The above
form indicates that under limited data conditions, controlling model
complexity, introducing
regularization constraints, and performing class balancing
\cite{goodfellow2016deep,Cui_2019_CVPR} are important theoretical means to
improve model stability.

\subsection{Performance Evaluation for Patch Classification}
\subsubsection{Overall and Class-Wise Metrics}
\par In patch-level focus detection tasks, model performance is reflected not
only in whether the overall classification is correct, but also in whether the
model can distinguish different classes of patches in a balanced way.
Therefore, experimental evaluation usually needs to examine overall accuracy,
class-wise precision and recall, and the confusion relations among different
classes at the same time \cite{schutze2008introduction}. For the binary and
three-class settings involved in this thesis, such metrics can reflect both the
model's ability to recognize clear regions and whether it tends to confuse
blurred, intermediate, or other categories with each other.

\par The most basic overall metric is accuracy. Suppose there are $N_v$ valid
patches in the validation set, with true labels $y_i$ and predicted labels
$\hat{y}_i$. Then accuracy is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Acc}=\frac{1}{N_v}\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}\mathbf{1}(\hat{y}_i=y_i)
    \label{eq:accuracy-basic}
\end{equation}
\par where $\mathbf{1}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. It takes the value
$1$ when the predicted label is equal to the true label, and $0$ otherwise.
Accuracy can summarize the overall correctness of the model on all samples, but
when the class distribution is imbalanced, relying on accuracy alone may hide
the performance decline of minority classes. Therefore, class-wise metrics are
usually needed for joint analysis.

\par For any class $c$, precision, recall, and $F_1$ score can be further
defined. Let the true positives, false positives, and false negatives of this
class be denoted by $TP_c$, $FP_c$, and $FN_c$, respectively. Then we have
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Precision}_c=\frac{TP_c}{TP_c+FP_c}
    \label{eq:precision-basic}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Recall}_c=\frac{TP_c}{TP_c+FN_c}
    \label{eq:recall-basic}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    F_{1,c}=
    \frac{2\cdot \operatorname{Precision}_c\cdot \operatorname{Recall}_c}
    {\operatorname{Precision}_c+\operatorname{Recall}_c}
    \label{eq:f1-basic}
\end{equation}
\par Precision, recall, and $F_1$ score \cite{schutze2008introduction} reflect,
respectively, how many samples predicted as a class truly belong to that class,
how many samples that truly belong to that class are successfully detected, and
the balance between the two. In focus detection tasks, if the clear class is
usually the minority class, recall is particularly important, because it can
directly reflect whether the model misses true clear regions. If it is further
necessary to compare class balance in a multi-class task from an overall
perspective, the arithmetic mean of the $F_1$ scores of all classes can be
taken to obtain macro $F_1$:
\begin{equation}
    F_1^{\mathrm{macro}}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}F_{1,c}
    \label{eq:macro-f1-basic}
\end{equation}
\par This metric assigns the same weight to each class. Therefore, when the
class distribution is imbalanced, it often reflects the true discrimination
ability of the model on minority classes better than overall accuracy alone
\cite{schutze2008introduction}.

\subsubsection{Confusion Analysis and Decision Rules}
\par To observe the misclassification patterns among classes more intuitively,
the confusion matrix is also commonly used \cite{schutze2008introduction}. For
a $C$-class classification problem, the element of the confusion matrix
$M\in\mathbb{N}^{C\times C}$ can be defined as
\begin{equation}
    M_{i,j}=
    \sum_{n=1}^{N_v}\mathbf{1}(y_n=i)\mathbf{1}(\hat{y}_n=j)
    \label{eq:confusion-basic}
\end{equation}
\par where $M_{i,j}$ denotes the number of patches whose true class is $i$ but
are predicted as $j$. The confusion matrix can reveal between which class
pairs the model's errors are mainly concentrated, and it is therefore important
for analyzing the difficulty difference between binary and three-class tasks.
For example, in a three-class task, the model may more often misclassify the
intermediate state as clear or blurred, rather than directly causing large-
scale confusion between clear and blurred.

\subsubsection{Performance-Cost Tradeoff}
\par For image patch classification tasks, the quality of a model is usually not
determined only by recognition metrics themselves, but is also related to the
computational cost required for training and inference. In general, a more
complex model may obtain stronger representation ability, but it is often
accompanied by higher training time, memory consumption, or parameter tuning
cost. A relatively simple model, although limited in expressive ability, may
still have advantages in reproducibility, deployment efficiency, and resource
consumption. Therefore, when comparing different methods, in addition to
performance metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, $F_1$, and macro
$F_1$, efficiency indicators such as training time are also commonly analyzed
together, so as to evaluate the practical applicability of a model more
comprehensively \cite{murphy2022pml,goodfellow2016deep}.
