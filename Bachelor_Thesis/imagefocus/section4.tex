\section{Experiments and Evaluation}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\par All experiments in this section are built on the unified pipeline described
in Section 3. \par In terms of data scale, the experiments in this thesis use
$350$ original images for training and another $75$ original images for
validation. These two sets are kept separate throughout the experiments. Since
the original resolutions are different, each image usually generates about
$2,000\sim3,000$ patches, and therefore the final training and validation are
performed on a large patch-level sample set. It should be noted that although
the number of patches is much larger than the number of original images, these
patches are not independently collected samples, but are obtained by splitting a
limited number of original images. Therefore, when reporting patch-level
experimental results, this thesis still keeps the number of original images as
an explanation of the data scale.

\subsection{Experimental Design}
\par All experiments in this chapter follow the same preprocessing, labeling,
and data-splitting pipeline described in Section 3. On this basis, the
experiments are organized into pre-experiments, main experiments, and
supplementary analyses. This design is intended to separate parameter selection,
final model comparison, and additional factor analysis, so that the overall
experimental logic remains clear.

\subsubsection{Pre-Experiments}
\par The pre-experiments are used to determine the unified parameter settings
for the later formal comparison. Specifically, they examine the threshold
selection for automatic labeling, the PCA retention ratio for the classical
machine learning models, and the number of training epochs for the CNN under
both the binary and three-class tasks. The selected settings are then fixed in
the subsequent main experiments.

\subsubsection{Main Experiments}
\par The main experiments compare the classical models and the lightweight CNN
under the binary and three-class tasks using the unified parameters determined
above. In addition to the final comparison under $100\%$ training samples, the
experiments also scan training sample ratios from $20\%$ to $100\%$ to analyze
the influence of sample size on model performance and stability.

\subsubsection{Supplementary Analyses}
\par The supplementary analyses are used to further interpret the main results.
They mainly examine the effect of weighted sampling in CNN training and the
effect of decision-threshold adjustment in binary CNN prediction. Throughout
these experiments, both classification metrics and training time are recorded to
evaluate the trade-off between recognition performance and computational cost.

\subsection{Pre-Experiments for Parameter Selection}
\subsubsection{Threshold Parameter Selection}
\par Before comparing different classifiers, this thesis first calibrates the
thresholds used in the automatic labeling stage. Since the subsequent supervised
learning task depends directly on the generated patch labels, the thresholds
need to produce a spatial distribution of blur, sharp, and intermediate regions
that is broadly consistent with human visual judgment of the focal plane.
Therefore, the threshold selection is treated as a pre-experimental step before
model training \cite{5739529,1284395}.

\par In this subsection, $T_b$ denotes the blur threshold and $T_s$ denotes the
sharp threshold in the fused sharpness score used for automatic labeling.

\par The calibration starts from the three-class setting. The reason is that the
three-class task provides a more explicit decomposition of local focus states:
patches are separated into blur, intermediate, and sharp regions, so the
appropriateness of the thresholds can be checked more directly through visual
inspection of the generated focus maps. In the initial trial, the blur and sharp
thresholds were set to $T_b=0.50$ and $T_s=0.85$. Under this setting, the
labeled maps showed that the blur region occupied too large an area, the
intermediate region was too limited, and the sharp region was also too small.
This indicated that the partition was too strict and could not reflect the human
judgment of the focal plane satisfactorily.

\par To further adjust the thresholds, this thesis manually checked nearly
$50\%$ of the training samples and compared the generated heatmaps and
three-class focus maps with the corresponding original images. Starting from the
initial setting, the thresholds were then increased or decreased in steps of
$5\%$ and multiple groups of visual comparison experiments were conducted. After
this iterative process, the final three-class setting was determined as
$T_b=0.40$ and $T_s=0.75$. Under this setting, the spatial distribution of blur,
intermediate, and sharp patches was judged to be more consistent with human
visual perception of focus transitions and focal regions.

\par After fixing the three-class thresholds, the sharp threshold $T_s=0.75$ was
further adopted as the decision boundary of the binary task. This choice is also
consistent with the labeling logic of the task itself. In the binary setting,
the intermediate and blur regions are merged into the same non-sharp category,
whereas only the sharp region is kept as an independent positive class.
Therefore, using $0.75$ as the binary threshold preserves the same sharp-region
judgment criterion as that used in the three-class setting, and the resulting
binary focus maps were also found to be visually consistent with human judgment.
Based on this calibration process, the subsequent experiments in this thesis use
$T_b=0.40$ and $T_s=0.75$ for the three-class task, and $T=0.75$ for the binary
task.
\par Although the threshold-based automatic labeling strategy provides an
interpretable and relatively consistent way to generate supervisory signals,
the approach still has several limitations. The thresholds are manually selected
and may remain sensitive to dataset-specific image characteristics and local
texture variations. In addition, fixed threshold rules may not fully capture
all complex focus transitions or ambiguous boundary regions. Therefore, the
automatic annotation procedure in this thesis is mainly intended to provide a
practical and repeatable label generation strategy rather than serving as a
final classification method by itself. The subsequent machine learning models
are still necessary to learn more robust decision boundaries from the patch
data and improve generalization performance across different image conditions.

\subsubsection{PCA Parameter Selection}

\par The feature dimension of a flattened original patch is $1,024$. Directly
using such high-dimensional features in classical classifiers increases the
training cost and may introduce redundant information
\cite{perry2025inference,zheng2021application}. Therefore, this thesis first
determines the PCA parameter used in the subsequent experiments through
comparative experiments. Under both the three-class and binary task settings,
identical decision tree, random forest, and Nystr\"{o}m-SVM configurations are
used to compare PCA retention ratios
\(\in\{0.95,0.99,0.995,0.999\}\). The evaluation considers accuracy, macro
precision, macro recall, macro F1, and training time on the validation set in
order to assess both classification performance and computational efficiency.

\subsubsection{Epoch Parameter Selection}

\par After determining the PCA parameter for the classical models, this thesis
further conducts pre-experiments on the number of CNN training epochs in order
to determine a unified epoch setting for the subsequent main experiments.
Specifically, under the three-class setting (sharp threshold $0.75$, blur
threshold $0.4$) and the binary setting (both sharp and blur thresholds set to
$0.75$), the influence of training epochs
\(\in\{10,20,30,50,60\}\) on validation performance and training time is
evaluated. The analysis considers accuracy, macro precision, macro recall,
macro F1, training time, and the convergence behaviour of the loss function to
assess both classification performance and computational efficiency under
different epoch settings.

\subsection{Main Experiments}

\par After completing the pre-experiments for PCA and epochs, this thesis
conducts the main experiments under a unified configuration. Specifically, the
classical models use a PCA retention ratio of $0.995$, the CNN is uniformly
trained for $30$ epochs, and the patch size is fixed at $32\times 32$. To
further investigate the influence of training scale on model performance, a
full scan over training sample ratios \(\{20,40,60,80,100\}\%\) is conducted
under both the binary setting (sharp and blur thresholds set to $0.75$) and
the three-class setting (sharp threshold $0.75$, blur threshold $0.4$). The
preprocessing time is $475.38$s for the binary task and $550.00$s for the
three-class task, indicating that the introduction of the intermediate class
increases data preparation complexity. The following analysis first compares
the final results under $100\%$ training samples and then further examines the
behaviour of different models as the training scale changes.

\par\medskip \noindent\textbf{Binary Setting.}
\par In the binary task, the validation set contains $121,405$ blur patches
and $14,665$ sharp patches, resulting in a clear class imbalance. Therefore,
the main comparison first evaluates the overall performance of the four models

Based on these overall metrics, a more detailed class-wise analysis is then
conducted using per-class results and confusion matrices.
Table~\ref{tab:main-binary-100} presents the overall experimental results under
$100\%$ training samples.
Although this overall comparison is based on all classes, the sharp class still
deserves particular attention in the subsequent detailed analysis, because the
practical objective of focus detection is to identify in-focus regions as
accurately as possible.

\par From the overall results, the CNN achieves the strongest overall
performance in the binary task, reaching the highest accuracy, macro
precision, and macro F1-score among the four models. This indicates that the
CNN provides the most effective balance between overall classification
performance and class-balanced evaluation metrics under the current setting.
Random forest and SVM also show strong overall performance, particularly in
macro recall, while the decision tree remains weaker on both overall and
class-balanced metrics.

\par However, the performance advantage of the CNN is accompanied by a
substantially higher computational cost. Under $100\%$ training samples, the
CNN requires $684.51$s of training time, whereas the SVM requires only
$17.68$s. This reflects a clear trade-off between computational efficiency and
marginal performance gain. Therefore, model selection should consider not only
classification performance, but also training efficiency and potential
deployment constraints.

\par At the class level, Table~\ref{tab:main-binary-perclass} shows that all
four models recognize the blur class relatively reliably, with blur-class
F1-scores remaining consistently high across models. The primary differences
appear in the detection behaviour of the minority sharp class. The CNN achieves
the highest precision and F1-score for the sharp class, but its recall remains
lower than that of random forest and SVM, indicating a more conservative
prediction strategy. In contrast, random forest and SVM achieve substantially
higher recall for the sharp class, but this comes at the cost of lower
precision, reflecting a more aggressive prediction behaviour. The decision tree
shows weaker performance on both overall and class-level metrics.

\par Figure~\ref{fig:cm-main-2c} further illustrates the error distributions of
the four models through the confusion matrices
\cite{schutze2008introduction}. Overall, all models distinguish blur and sharp
patches relatively well, but they differ substantially in the balance between
false positives and false negatives. The CNN produces the smallest number of
false positives, indicating that it predicts the sharp class only under high
confidence. This conservative behaviour improves precision but also increases
the number of missed sharp patches. In contrast, random forest and SVM reduce
false negatives more effectively, leading to higher recall for the sharp class
at the expense of more false positives. The decision tree exhibits larger
errors in both directions, which explains its weaker overall performance.

\par Therefore, in the binary task, the CNN achieves the strongest overall
performance, whereas the SVM provides the best computational efficiency and
random forest offers a balanced trade-off between performance and training
cost.

\par Compared with the binary task, the three-class task is substantially more
challenging. After introducing the intermediate class, the models must not
only separate sharp and blur patches, but also handle transition regions with
more ambiguous visual characteristics. As a result, the overall macro
F1-scores are lower than those observed in the binary setting.

\par From the overall metrics, the SVM shows the strongest overall balance in
the three-class task, achieving the highest accuracy and macro F1-score while
maintaining stable macro precision and macro recall. Random forest follows
closely and also achieves strong class-balanced performance. By comparison, the
decision tree and CNN remain weaker on the overall metrics under this setting.

\par At the class level, Table~\ref{tab:main-triclass-perclass} shows that all
models recognize the blur class relatively stably, while the main differences

minority-class detection capability. However, this advantage is accompanied by
lower precision, suggesting a more aggressive prediction strategy. In contrast,
the CNN maintains extremely high precision for the sharp class, but its recall
decreases substantially, indicating that many real sharp patches are instead
assigned to the intermediate class.

\par Among the four models, the SVM provides the most balanced class-wise
performance. In particular, it achieves the highest F1-score for the
intermediate class while maintaining stable performance across all categories.
This balanced error structure is also consistent with its superior macro
F1-score in the overall evaluation.

classification difficulty after introducing the intermediate class. Overall,
the main source of error is not the direct confusion between sharp and blur
patches, but the ambiguity between the sharp and intermediate classes.

\par The confusion matrices further reveal different prediction behaviours
among the models. The CNN forms a highly conservative decision boundary and
predicts the sharp class only under high confidence, causing many real sharp
patches to be absorbed into the intermediate class. Random forest adopts a
more aggressive strategy, achieving higher recall for the sharp class while
simultaneously increasing the number of intermediate patches misclassified as
sharp. Compared with these two extremes, the SVM exhibits a more balanced
error structure, which contributes to its strong overall stability and
class-balanced performance in the three-class task.

\par Overall, the experimental results show that different models exhibit
different trade-offs between class-balanced performance, minority-class
detection, and computational efficiency. In the binary task, the CNN achieves
the strongest overall performance, although at substantially higher
computational cost. In the three-class task, the SVM demonstrates the most
stable and balanced behaviour across all classes, while random forest provides
stronger minority-class recall at the expense of lower precision. These results
indicate that the introduction of the intermediate class significantly
increases classification difficulty and makes the balance between recall and
precision more critical for model evaluation.
\subsection{Sample-Size Analysis}

\par To analyze the role of training data scale more systematically, this
section treats the scan result from $20\%$ to $100\%$ samples as an independent
dimension for discussion. Compared with only comparing the final results under a
single training ratio, sample-size analysis can better reveal the speed of
learning saturation, the sensitivity of models to data volume, and whether the
performance improvement is worth the additional computational cost.

\subsubsection{Class Distribution and Scaling}

\par From the training data distribution, the binary task keeps a stable
imbalanced structure across all five training ratios. Taking the $100\%$
training set as an example, the numbers of non-sharp and sharp samples are
$578,212$ and $59,941$, respectively, and the sharp class accounts for only
about $9.39\%$. In the $20\%$ training set, the corresponding numbers are
$115,735$ and $11,896$, and the ratio remains nearly unchanged. This indicates
that changing the training sample ratio mainly affects the total sample number
rather than the class proportion. Therefore, the training sample quantity
becomes the main controlled factor in the sample-size comparison.

\par The three-class task shows a similar stable distribution. Under the
$100\%$ training set, the blur, sharp, and intermediate classes contain
$320,810$, $59,941$, and $257,402$ samples, respectively. The sharp class still
accounts for only about $9.39\%$, while the blur and intermediate classes
account for about $50.27\%$ and $40.34\%$, respectively. This means that the
three-class task not only involves minority-class detection, but also requires
the model to establish a stable boundary between two large visually related
classes, making the classification problem more challenging than the binary
setting.

limited. Most curves show only slight fluctuation or slow improvement, which

data distribution, increasing sample size can provide some benefit but is not
the dominant factor determining final model performance. Instead, model
decision behaviour, class-boundary complexity, and the balance between
precision and recall appear to influence the final results more directly.

sample ratio. Therefore, when performance gain remains limited, the value of
further enlarging the training set must be evaluated together with the

on whether the observed overall trends are caused by stable majority-class

\subsection{Supplementary Analyses}
\par In addition to the main experiments, this thesis further investigates the
role of sample rebalancing strategies in the CNN. It should be clarified that
the CNN in the main experiments of the thesis already uses loss weights
calculated according to class frequency, but does not enable weighted sampling.
Therefore, the ``weighted sampler'' discussed in this section more precisely
means enabling an additional weighted sampling strategy while keeping the loss
weights unchanged, and observing whether this can further improve the
recognition performance of the minority sharp class
\cite{Cui_2019_CVPR,buda2018systematic}. Since this supplementary experiment is
only conducted on the CNN, the results of the classical models remain unchanged.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Supplementary comparison before and after enabling the weighted sampler in CNN}
    \label{tab:supp-weighted-sampler}
    \begin{tabular}{llccccc}
        \toprule
        Task & Setting & Accuracy & Precision$_{\mathrm{focus}}$ &
        Recall$_{\mathrm{focus}}$ & F1$_{\mathrm{focus}}$ & Macro F1 \\
        \midrule
        Binary & No sampler (main exp.)     & 0.9709 & 0.99 & 0.73 & 0.84 & 0.91
        \\
        Binary & Weighted sampler, $t=0.5$  & 0.9666 & 1.00 & 0.69 & 0.82 & 0.90
        \\
        Binary & Weighted sampler, $t=0.3$  & 0.9697 & 1.00 & 0.72 & 0.84 & 0.91
        \\
        Three-class & No sampler (main exp.)    & 0.8709 & 1.00 & 0.60 & 0.75 &
        0.83 \\
        Three-class & Weighted sampler          & 0.8725 & 1.00 & 0.69 & 0.82 &
        0.85 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\par From the binary results, the weighted sampler does not directly bring the
expected gain. When the default threshold $t=0.5$ is retained, the precision of
the model on the sharp class remains at $1.00$, which indicates that the
prediction strategy is still very conservative. However, recall drops from
$0.73$ to $0.69$, which causes the sharp-class F1-score to fall back from $0.84$
to $0.82$. At the same time, the overall macro F1 also decreases from $0.91$
to $0.90$. This shows that
under the binary setting, the loss weight in the main experiment is already
sufficient to impose a strong constraint on the minority class, and the
additional introduction of sampling reweighting does not further improve sharp-
class detection, but may instead enlarge training fluctuation. Furthermore,
after lowering the decision threshold of the sharp class in the supplementary
experiment from $0.5$ to $0.3$, the sharp-class recall rises back to $0.72$, the
sharp-class F1-score recovers to $0.84$, and the overall macro F1 also returns
to $0.91$.
This indicates that the main role of the weighted sampler in binary
classification is not to stably improve representation ability, but to change
the output score distribution. If threshold calibration is not used together
with it, the gain is not obvious \cite{davis2006relationship}.

\par The three-class result shows a different conclusion. After enabling the
weighted sampler, the sharp-class recall of the CNN clearly increases from
$0.60$ to $0.69$, and the sharp-class F1-score rises from $0.75$ to $0.82$.
At the same time, macro F1 improves from $0.83$ to $0.85$, while accuracy also
increases slightly. This indicates that under the three-class setting, sample reweighting
does indeed alleviate the learning pressure faced by the sharp class as a
minority class, and makes the model no longer as overly conservative as in the
main experiment. Combined with the confusion matrix, it can be seen that the
weighted sampler mainly reduces the number of real sharp-class samples that are
predicted as intermediate-state, so the improvement is not only a fluctuation of
local metrics, but a direct improvement on the most difficult class boundary.

\par Overall, the effect of the weighted sampler is clearly task-dependent. For
the binary task, additional sampling reweighting is not better than the main
experimental configuration, which indicates that ``loss weight + default
threshold'' is already sufficient. If the CNN is to be further improved later,
threshold calibration should be considered before further strengthening sampling
bias. For the three-class task, however, the weighted sampler brings a
substantial gain, which indicates that when the intermediate-state class is
introduced, the learning of the minority sharp patches by the CNN is more easily
suppressed by majority-class boundaries, and in this case strengthening
minority-class exposure through sampling rebalance is effective. Therefore, if
future work continues to focus on the three-class CNN, the weighted sampler is
recommended as a default training option rather than relying only on static loss
weights.

\subsection{Results}
\par By combining the pre-experiments, main experiments, supplementary analyses,
and manual verification results in this section, several overall results can be
summarized. First, the binary task is consistently easier than the three-class
task and yields higher and more stable performance. Second, the manual
verification results are generally consistent with the quantitative comparison,
which supports the validity of the observed differences among models. Third, the
relative advantages of the models differ across task settings: in the binary
task, the CNN achieves the strongest overall performance according to accuracy
and macro F1, whereas in the three-class task, the SVM provides the most stable
macro-level performance. The sharp-class results are therefore used as a
detailed class-level interpretation rather than as the sole basis for model
selection.

\par The pre-experimental results further verify the rationality of the unified
parameter setting used in the subsequent main experiments. For the classical
models, although a PCA retention ratio of $0.999$ can obtain higher metrics in
some individual experiments, the gain lacks consistency, and the training time
of decision tree and random forest increases clearly. By comparison, a PCA
retention ratio of $0.995$ shows a more balanced performance-cost trade-off in
both binary and three-class tasks. For the CNN, uniformly using $30$ training
epochs reflects the same principle. Compared with $20$ epochs, this setting can
further push loss convergence and bring higher recall and F1-score in the binary
task. When the number of epochs continues to increase to $50$ or $60$, the
training cost grows significantly, but the performance gain is already very
limited. Therefore, the parameter selection in this section does not simply
pursue the highest metric in a single experiment, but emphasizes stability,
consistency, and reproducibility across tasks and models.

\par Figures~\ref{fig:pca-2d-visualization} and \ref{fig:pca-3d-visualization}
further show the PCA visualization results of the same sample under the binary
and three-class settings. Although some overlap between classes is still
present, the overall clustering trend is already observable, especially the
separation between sharp and blur samples in principal component space. Under
the three-class setting, the intermediate class is mainly distributed in the
transition region between the other two classes, which is consistent with the
labeling logic adopted in this thesis. These results indicate that the
patch-level representation after preprocessing and labeling already has
preliminary separability, providing a reasonable basis for the subsequent
classifier training.

\par The main and supplementary results show clear differences among models
under the two task settings. In the binary task, the CNN achieves the strongest
overall performance, while the SVM maintains high macro recall with relatively
low training cost, and random forest provides a competitive intermediate
result. In the three-class task, the SVM keeps the most stable overall
performance, whereas random forest is more advantageous in sharp-class
detection. Overall, the binary task is easier than the three-class task and
yields higher and more stable results.

\par The sample-size analysis further shows that increasing the training ratio
from $20\%$ to $100\%$ brings only limited metric improvement for most models,
while training time increases steadily. The supplementary experiments show
different behaviors across task settings: in the binary CNN, weighted sampling
does not improve the main result, whereas in the three-class CNN it improves
sharp-class recall and F1-score. These results indicate that performance in
patch-based focus detection is jointly affected by task definition, class-
boundary complexity, imbalance handling, and computational cost.
